\section{Definition 3.6: The dual map}
Let \(A:U\to V\) be linear and $y\in V'$. Its \emph{dual map} is
\[
 A':V'\longrightarrow U',\qquad A'y=y\circ A,
 \quad\text{meaning}\quad (A'y)(u)=y(Au).
\]
The direction reverses: \(A\) maps vectors from \(U\) to \(V\), whereas \(A'\) maps linear functionals on \(V\) to linear functionals on \(U\).

\section{Theorem 3.8: Equality of ranks}
\textbf{Statement.} For every linear map \(A:U\to V\),
\[
 \boxed{\rank(A')=\rank(A).}
\]
Here \(\rank(A)=\dim\Ran(A)\), with \(\Ran(A)=A(U)\)**can we just say that?.

\textbf{Proof.} Define
\[
 S:\Ran(A)'\longrightarrow U',\qquad Sf=f\circ A,
\]
where \(f\) is a linear functional on the subspace \(\Ran(A)\subseteq V\).
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \textbf{\(S\) is injective.} If \(Sf=0\), then \(f(Au)=0\) **why? for all \(u\in U\). Every \(v\in\Ran(A)\) equals \(Au\) for some \(u\), so \(f(v)=0\) throughout \(\Ran(A)\). Hence \(f=0\).
\item \textbf{\(\Ran(A')\subseteq\Ran(S)\).} Given \(A'y=y\circ A\), restrict \(y\in V'\) to \(\Ran(A)\): let \(f=y|_{\Ran(A)}\). Then \(Sf=f\circ A=y\circ A=A'y\).
\item \textbf{\(\Ran(S)\subseteq\Ran(A')\).} For \(f\in\Ran(A)'\), Lemma 3.4(1) (a functional on a subspace extends to the whole space) supplies \(y\in V'\) with \(y|_{\Ran(A)}=f\). Thus \(Sf=y\circ A=A'y\).
\item Therefore \(S:\Ran(A)'\to\Ran(A')\) is a linear bijection. Using Theorem 3.5 (a vector space and its dual have the same dimension),
\[
 \rank(A')=\dim\Ran(A')=\dim\Ran(A)'=\dim\Ran(A)=\rank(A).
\]
\end{proofsteps}
\begin{personalnote}
Do not confuse \(\Ran(A)'\) with \(\Ran(A')\): the first is the dual of the image of \(A\); the second is the image of the dual map \(A'\). The map \(S\) identifies them.
\end{personalnote}

\section{Lemma 3.25: Matrix of the dual map}
Choose ordered bases \(u_1,\ldots,u_m\) of \(U\) and \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\) of \(V\), and corresponding dual bases \(z_1,\ldots,z_m\) of \(U'\) and \(y_1,\ldots,y_n\) of \(V'\).

\textbf{Statement.} Relative to these bases,
\[
 \boxed{[A']=[A]^t.}
\]

\textbf{Proof.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item Write the images of basis elements in coordinates:
\[
 Au_j=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{ij}x_i,\qquad
 A'y_l=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\beta_{kl}z_k.
\]
The matrices have entries \([A]_{ij}=\alpha_{ij}\) and \([A']_{kl}=\beta_{kl}\).
\item Apply \(y_l\) to the first identity. Since \(y_l(x_i)=\delta_{li}\),
\[
 y_l(Au_j)=\sum_i\alpha_{ij}y_l(x_i)=\alpha_{lj}.
\]
\item Evaluate the second identity at \(u_j\). Since \(z_k(u_j)=\delta_{kj}\),
\[
 (A'y_l)(u_j)=\sum_k\beta_{kl}z_k(u_j)=\beta_{jl}.
\]
\item By Definition 3.6 (the dual map acts by precomposition),
\[
 \alpha_{lj}=y_l(Au_j)=(A'y_l)(u_j)=\beta_{jl}.
\]
Thus the row and column indices swap, so \([A']=[A]^t\).
\end{proofsteps}
\begin{personalnote}
The \(j\)-th column of \([A]\) gives the coordinates of \(Au_j\). A dual basis functional \(y_l\) extracts the \(l\)-th coordinate because \(y_l(x_i)=\delta_{li}\). Dual basis elements are \emph{linear functionals}, not vectors in \(V\).
\end{personalnote}

\section{Corollary 3.26: Row rank equals column rank}
\textbf{Statement.} For every matrix \(M\), its row rank and column rank are equal.

\textbf{Proof.} Let \(M\in\F^{n\times m}\) represent \(A:\F^m\to\F^n\), \(A(x)=Mx\). The range of \(A\) is the span of the columns of \(M\), since \(Mx\) is a linear combination of those columns. By Theorem 3.8 (a map and its dual have equal rank) and Lemma 3.25 (the dual map is represented by the transpose),
\[
 \operatorname{columnrank}(M)
 =\rank(A)=\rank(A')
 =\operatorname{columnrank}(M^t)
 =\operatorname{rowrank}(M).
\]
The last equality holds because columns of \(M^t\) are rows of \(M\).

\begin{personalnote}
The \emph{column space} is the span of the columns, and its dimension is the \emph{column rank}. Similarly, the row rank is the dimension of the span of the rows. For \(A(x)=Mx\), the range \(\Ran(A)\) equals the column space.
\end{personalnote}

Lemma 3.25 & Identify \([A']\) with \(M^t\).\\
Transpose & Columns of \(M^t\) are rows of \(M\).\\\hline

\section{Big Picture: How to present Topic A}
Start with a linear map \(A:U\to V\). Its dual \(A':V'\to U'\) sends \(y\) to \(y\circ A\), reversing direction. Lemma 3.7 shows that dualization is compatible with linear structure, composition and inverses.

Theorem 3.8 establishes the central abstract fact: \(A\) and \(A'\) have the same rank. The key construction is the isomorphism \(S:\Ran(A)'\to\Ran(A')\), obtained by composing functionals with \(A\). Restriction and extension of functionals prove that it is onto.

Finally choose bases. Lemma 3.25 turns abstract duality into a matrix statement: the matrix of \(A'\) is the transpose of that of \(A\). Corollary 3.26 follows: column rank equals row rank.

\[
 \boxed{\text{Dual map}\ \longrightarrow\
 \rank(A)=\rank(A')\ \longrightarrow\
 [A']=[A]^t\ \longrightarrow\
 \text{row rank}=\text{column rank}.}
\]

\part*{Topic C: Tensor Products}

\section*{Definition 5.5: Universal property}
A tensor product of the vector spaces $X$ and $Y$ is a pair \((T,B)\), with \(B:X\times Y\to T\) bilinear, such that for every vector space \(U\) and bilinear map \(M:X\times Y\to U\), there exists a \emph{unique} linear map \(L:T\to U\) satisfying \(M=L\circ B\):
\[
\begin{array}{ccc}X\times Y&\xrightarrow{\ B\ }&T\\&\searrow_{\scriptstyle M}&\downarrow{\scriptstyle\exists!L}\\&&U.\end{array}
\]

We write \(T=X\otimes Y\), \(B(x,y)=x\otimes y\); these are \emph{pure tensors}.
\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
\textbf{Bilinear:} a map \(M:X\times Y\to U\) that is linear in each variable separately, with the other held fixed.
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Theorem 5.7: Existence (Theorem 5.6(a))}
For any pair of vector spaces X and Y, there exists a pair $(T,B)$ such that it's a tensor product of $X$ and $Y$.

\textbf{Proof.} For each \(x\in X\), \(y\in Y\), define an evaluation functional
\(x\otimes y\in\operatorname{Bil}(X,Y;\F)'\) by
\[
(x\otimes y)(w)=w(x,y)\qquad(w\in\operatorname{Bil}(X,Y;\F)).
\]
Set \(T=\operatorname{Span}\{x\otimes y:x\in X,\ y\in Y\}\subseteq\operatorname{Bil}(X,Y;\F)'\) and \(B(x,y)=x\otimes y\).**T is a vector spaceeeeee.
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \(B\) is bilinear: evaluate \((ax_1+bx_2)\otimes y\) at any \(w\in\operatorname{Bil}(X,Y;\F)\), obtaining
\(w(ax_1+bx_2,y)=aw(x_1,y)+bw(x_2,y)\)**wite it out in tensor products; similarly in the second argument.
\item Given bilinear \(M:X\times Y\to U\), UP forces the following formula (since \(M=L\circ B\) requires \(L(x\otimes y)=M(x,y)\), and \(L\) must be linear):
\[
L\Big(\sum_i a_ix_i\otimes y_i\Big)=\sum_i a_iM(x_i,y_i). \tag{*}
\]
Since every element of \(T\) is a linear combination of pure tensors, the formula prescribes \(L\) on \emph{every} element of \(T\). Thus any linear map satisfying \(L(x\otimes y)=M(x,y)\) must use this formula; there can be at most one such \(L\). We still have to check that different representations of the same tensor give the same result.
\item \textbf{Well-definedness.} Suppose \(\sum_i a_ix_i\otimes y_i=\sum_j b_ju_j\otimes v_j\). For any \(z\in U'\), the composition \(w=z\circ M\) belongs to \(\operatorname{Bil}(X,Y;\F)\). Evaluate the tensor equality at \(w\):
\[
z\Big(\sum_i a_iM(x_i,y_i)\Big)
=\sum_i a_iw(x_i,y_i)
=\sum_j b_jw(u_j,v_j)
=z\Big(\sum_j b_jM(u_j,v_j)\Big).
\]
By Lemma 3.3(1) (linear functionals distinguish nonzero vectors), the two sums in \(U\) agree. Thus (*) is independent of representation.
\item Formula (*) therefore defines linear \(L:T\to U\) with \(L(x\otimes y)=M(x,y)\), establishing UP.
\end{proofsteps}
\begin{personalnote}
Here \(x\otimes y\) is a functional \emph{on the space of bilinear forms}: it sends \(w\) to \(w(x,y)\).
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Theorem 5.6(b): Uniqueness}
\textbf{Statement.} If \((T,B)\) and \((S,A)\) are tensor products of \(X,Y\), there is a unique isomorphism \(\Psi:T\to S\) with \(\Psi B=A\).**make me a drawing

\section*{Lemma 5.8: Pure tensors span}
\textbf{Statement.} \(X\otimes Y=\operatorname{Span}\{x\otimes y\mid x\in X,y\in Y \}\).

\textbf{Proof.} Put \(T=X\otimes Y\), \(T_0=\operatorname{Span}\{x\otimes y\mid x\in X,y\in Y\}\), and let \(Q:T\to T/T_0\) be the quotient map. Since every \(B(x,y)=x\otimes y\) lies in \(T_0\), we have \(QB=0\). The zero map also satisfies \(0B=0\). By uniqueness in UP, \(Q=0\), so \(T=T_0\).
The two maps \(Q\) and \(0\) both complete the following diagram (the diagonal map is the zero bilinear map):
\[
\begin{array}{ccc}
X\times Y&\xrightarrow{\ B\ }&T=X\otimes Y\\
&\searrow_{\scriptstyle 0}&\downarrow\scriptstyle{Q\ \text{or}\ 0}\\
&&T/T_0.
\end{array}
\]
Uniqueness in UP therefore gives \(Q=0\).
\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
\textbf{Tensor product \(X\otimes Y\):} the vector space constructed from \(X\) and \(Y\) with the universal property. The symbol \(\otimes\) here names the entire vector space, not one tensor.\\
\textbf{Tensor:} any element of \(X\otimes Y\).\\
\textbf{Pure tensor:} an element of the special form \(x\otimes y\) for a single pair \((x,y)\). Every tensor is a \emph{finite sum} of pure tensors (Lemma 5.8), but not every tensor is itself pure. For example, in \(\mathbb R^2\otimes\mathbb R^2\), \(e_1\otimes e_1+e_2\otimes e_2\) is not pure.
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Lemma 5.9: Independence test}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(y_1,\ldots,y_k\) are linearly independent and \(\sum_i x_i\otimes y_i=0\), then all \(x_i=0\). \textcolor{red}{In particular, \(x\otimes y=0\) iff \(x=0\) or \(y=0\).}

\textbf{Proof.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item For arbitrary \(z\in X'\), define bilinear \(M(x,y)=z(x)y:X\times Y\to Y\).
\item UP gives linear \(L:X\otimes Y\to Y\) with \(L(x\otimes y)=z(x)y\).
\item Hence \(0=L(\sum_i x_i\otimes y_i)=\sum_i z(x_i)y_i\). Independence of the \(y_i\) forces \(z(x_i)=0\).
\item Since this holds for every \(z\in X'\), Lemma 3.3(1) (a nonzero vector is detected by some functional) implies \(x_i=0\). \textcolor{red}{The special case follows by taking \(k=1\) if \(y\neq0\); the converse follows from bilinearity.}
\end{proofsteps}

\section*{Corollary 5.10: Independent pure tensors}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(C\subseteq X,D\subseteq Y\) are linearly independent subsets, then 
\newline\(C\otimes D=\{x\otimes y:x\in C,y\in D\}\) is linearly independent and \(C\times D\to C\otimes D\) is bijective.

\[
0=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}x_i\otimes y_j
=\sum_j\Big(\sum_i a_{ij}x_i\Big)\otimes y_j.
\]

\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
A subset \(C\subseteq X\) is \textbf{linearly independent} if, for every choice of \emph{distinct} vectors \(x_1,\ldots,x_k\in C\) and scalars \(a_1,\ldots,a_k\in\F\),
\[
\sum_{i=1}^k a_ix_i=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_1=\cdots=a_k=0.

\section*{\textcolor{red}{Theorem 5.11: Tensor-product basis}}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(C,D\) are bases of \(X,Y\), then \(C\otimes D\) is a basis of \(X\otimes Y\). If finite-dimensional,
\[
\boxed{\dim(X\otimes Y)=\dim(X)\dim(Y).}
\]
\textbf{Proof.} By Corollary 5.10 (products of independent sets are independent), \(C\otimes D\) is independent. For \(x=\sum_i a_ix_i\) and \(y=\sum_j b_jy_j\), bilinearity gives
\[
x\otimes y=\sum_{i,j}a_ib_j(x_i\otimes y_j)\in\operatorname{Span}(C\otimes D).
\]
By Lemma 5.8 (pure tensors span the tensor product), this spans all of \(X\otimes Y\). Thus it is a basis. If \(|C|=m,|D|=n\), it has \(mn\) distinct elements, giving the dimension formula.

\part*{Topic D: Minimal Polynomial and Diagonalizability}

\section*{Definitions for understanding}
Let \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)=\operatorname{Hom}(V,V)\). For a polynomial
\(f(X)=\sum_{i=0}^n a_iX^i\in\F[X]\), define
\[
f(A)=\sum_{i=0}^n a_iA^i\in\operatorname{End}(V),\qquad A^0=I.
\]
Thus \(f(A)\) is a \emph{linear map}, not a scalar; for example
\(f(X)=2X+3X^3\) gives \(f(A)=2A+3A^3\).

A polynomial \emph{annihilates} \(A\) when \(f(A)=0\), the zero map.

A \emph{minimal polynomial} \(p_A\) is a nonzero monic polynomial of smallest
degree satisfying \(p_A(A)=0\). Monic means that its leading coefficient is \(1\).

\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
\(\operatorname{Hom}(U,V)\) denotes linear maps \(U\to V\), and
\(\operatorname{End}(V)=\operatorname{Hom}(V,V)\).

The expression \(A^i\) means composition of \(A\) with itself \(i\) times.
The constant term \(a_0\) in \(f(A)\) means \(a_0I\).

Do not confuse the nonzero polynomial \(p_A(X)\) with its value \(p_A(A)=0\).
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Theorem 6.13: Existence and uniqueness}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(\dim V<\infty\), every \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\)
has exactly one minimal polynomial.

\textbf{Proof.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \textbf{Existence.} Suppose that \(A^m\notin\operatorname{Span}\{I,A,\ldots,A^{m-1}\}\) for every \(m\geq1\). By Lemma 1.11 and induction, the maps \(I,A,A^2,\ldots\) would then be linearly independent in \(\operatorname{End}(V)\). But \(\operatorname{End}(V)\) is finite-dimensional, so it cannot contain infinitely many linearly independent maps. Therefore, choose the smallest \(m\geq1\) such that
\[
A^m\in\operatorname{Span}\{I,A,\ldots,A^{m-1}\}.
\]
Writing \(A^m=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_iA^i\), we get \(p(A)=0\) for the monic polynomial
\[
p(X)=X^m-\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}a_iX^i.
\]
The minimal choice of \(m\) ensures that no nonzero polynomial of smaller degree annihilates \(A\). Thus \(p\) is a minimal polynomial.
\item \textbf{Uniqueness.} If \(p,q\) are monic minimal polynomials,
they have the same degree \(m\). Their leading terms cancel in \(p-q\),
and \((p-q)(A)=p(A)-q(A)=0-0=0\), since both \(p\) and \(q\) annihilate \(A\). If \(p-q\neq0\), it would have degree less than \(m\),
contradicting minimality. Thus \(p=q\).
\end{proofsteps}

\begin{personalnote}
Here we treat the maps \(I,A,A^2,\ldots\)
as \emph{vectors in the vector space of linear maps}.
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Lemma 6.11: Divisibility}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(p_A\) is minimal and \(P(A)=0\), then \(p_A\mid P\).

\textbf{Proof.} Euclidean division (Theorem 6.5) gives
\[
P(X)=p_A(X)q(X)+r(X),\qquad r=0\ \text{or}\ \deg r<\deg p_A.
\]
Evaluate at \(A\):
\[
r(A)=P(A)-p_A(A)q(A)=0.
\]
If \(r\neq0\), this is a nonzero polynomial of degree less than \(p_A\)
that annihilates \(A\), contradicting minimality. Thus \(r=0\), so
\(P=p_Aq\), i.e.\ \(p_A\mid P\).

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is the remainder zero?} Euclidean division only tells us
its degree is smaller. Evaluating at \(A\) shows it also annihilates \(A\).
The \emph{minimality} of \(p_A\) then forces \(r\) to be the zero polynomial.
Be careful: it is \(p_A(A)=0\), not necessarily \(q(A)=0\).
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Lemma 6.12: Roots are eigenvalues}
\textbf{Statement.} \(Z(p_A)=\sigma(A)\), where \(Z(p_A)\) is the set of roots
and \(\sigma(A)\) the set of eigenvalues.

\textbf{Proof.} Let \(\lambda\in Z(p_A)\). The factor theorem gives
\[
p_A(X)=(X-\lambda)q(X),\qquad \deg q=\deg p_A-1.
\]
By minimality, \(q(A)\neq0\). Choose \(x\in V\) with
\(y=q(A)x\neq0\). Then
\[
(A-\lambda I)y=(A-\lambda I)q(A)x=p_A(A)x=0.
\]
Thus \(Ay=\lambda y\) with \(y\neq0\), so \(\lambda\in\sigma(A)\).
Conversely, Lemma 6.9 says that if \(Ax=\lambda x\) for some \(x\neq0\), then \(f(A)x=f(\lambda)x\) for every polynomial \(f\). Applying this with \(f=p_A\), we have \(0=p_A(A)x=p_A(\lambda)x\). As \(x\neq0\), \(p_A(\lambda)=0\), so \(\sigma(A)\subseteq Z(p_A)\).

\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
\textbf{Eigenvalue:} \(\lambda\in\F\) for which some \emph{nonzero}
\(y\in V\) satisfies \(Ay=\lambda y\).
\(\sigma(A)=\{\lambda:\lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue}\}\);
\(Z(p_A)=\{\lambda:p_A(\lambda)=0\}\).
\end{personalnote}
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why does \(q(A)\neq0\) give an eigenvector?}
A nonzero linear map must send \emph{some} vector \(x\) to a nonzero vector;
it need not send every vector to something nonzero.
We deliberately choose \(y=q(A)x\neq0\).
The factorization ensures \((A-\lambda I)y=0\), so this \(y\) is an eigenvector.
\textbf{Why factor?} Since \(p_A(\lambda)=0\), the factor theorem says
\(X-\lambda\) divides \(p_A\).
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Corollary 6.14: Existence of eigenvalues}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(0<\dim V<\infty\) and \(\F\) is algebraically
closed, every \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\) has an eigenvalue.

\textbf{Proof.} By Theorem 6.13, \(A\) has a minimal polynomial \(p_A\).
It is nonconstant: the only monic constant polynomial is \(1\), but
\(1(A)=I\neq0\) since \(V\neq\{0\}\).
As \(\F\) is algebraically closed, \(p_A\) has a root \(\lambda\in\F\).
Lemma 6.12 then makes \(\lambda\) an eigenvalue.
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why assume algebraic closure?}
A minimal polynomial does \emph{not} necessarily split over an arbitrary field. For instance, over \(\mathbb R\), the rotation matrix with minimal polynomial \(X^2+1\) has no real eigenvalues. Algebraic closure guarantees that every nonconstant polynomial has a root in \(\mathbb F\), and in fact splits into linear factors. For this corollary, the existence of one root is sufficient.
\end{personalnote}

\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
A field is \textbf{algebraically closed} if every nonconstant polynomial
over it has a root in that field. For example, \(\mathbb C\) is algebraically
closed, whereas \(\mathbb R\) is not: \(X^2+1\) has no real root.
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Theorem 6.18: Minimal polynomial and diagonalizability}
Assume \(\dim V<\infty\), with distinct eigenvalues
\(\sigma(A)=\{\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m\}\).

\textbf{Statement.} \(A\) is diagonalizable if and only if \(p_A\)
splits into linear factors without repeated roots. In that case
\[
\text{(i)}\quad p_A(X)=\prod_{i=1}^m(X-\lambda_i),\qquad
\text{(ii)}\quad V=\bigoplus_{i=1}^m V_{\lambda_i},
\]
and the associated projections onto \(V_{\lambda_i}\) are
\(E_i=p_i(A)\), with the Lagrange polynomials
\[
p_i(X)=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{X-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}.
\]
By Lemma 6.12, the roots of \(p_A\) are exactly the eigenvalues;
thus (i) is equivalent to splitting without repeated roots.
We prove \(\text{diagonalizable}\Rightarrow\text{(i)}
\Rightarrow\text{(ii)}\Rightarrow\text{diagonalizable}\).

\textbf{Proof.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \textbf{Diagonalizable \(\Rightarrow\) (i).}
Let \(q(X)=\prod_{i=1}^m(X-\lambda_i)\).
For an eigenvector \(x\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\),
\((A-\lambda_iI)x=0\), hence \(q(A)x=0\).
Since eigenvectors form a basis of \(V\), linearity gives \(q(A)=0\): if \(v=\sum_k c_kx_k\) is expressed in an eigenvector basis, then \(q(A)v=\sum_k c_kq(A)x_k=0\).
Lemma 6.11 implies \(p_A\mid q\).
By Lemma 6.12, each distinct \(\lambda_i\) is a root of \(p_A\),
so \(q\mid p_A\). Since \(p_A\mid q\) and \(q\mid p_A\), we have \(p_A=cq\) for some nonzero scalar \(c\). Both polynomials are monic, so comparing leading coefficients gives \(c=1\); hence \(p_A=q\).

\item \textbf{(i) \(\Rightarrow\) (ii).}
Assume \(p_A(X)=\prod_{k=1}^m(X-\lambda_k)\), with distinct \(\lambda_k\). For each \(i\), define the Lagrange polynomial
\[
p_i(X)=\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{X-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j},
\qquad E_i=p_i(A),\qquad U_i=R(E_i).
\]
Here \(E_i\) is a linear map obtained by substituting \(A\) into \(p_i\), and \(U_i\) is its image. We will prove that \(U_i\) is exactly the eigenspace \(V_{\lambda_i}\).

\textbf{Step 1: The maps sum to the identity.}
Lemma 6.17 gives the Lagrange identity \(\sum_{i=1}^m p_i(X)=1\). Evaluating this polynomial identity at \(A\) yields
\[
\sum_{i=1}^m E_i=\sum_{i=1}^m p_i(A)=I.
\]
Thus for every \(v\in V\), \(v=\sum_iE_iv\), with \(E_iv\in U_i\). In particular, the images \(U_i\) span \(V\).

\textbf{Step 2: Different maps annihilate one another.}
For \(i\neq j\), the polynomial \(p_i\) contains all factors \(X-\lambda_k\) except \(X-\lambda_i\), while \(p_j\) contains all except \(X-\lambda_j\). Together, their product contains \emph{every} factor of \(p_A\). Hence \(p_A\mid p_ip_j\): there is a polynomial \(h\) with \(p_ip_j=p_Ah\). Consequently,
\[
E_iE_j=p_i(A)p_j(A)=(p_ip_j)(A)=p_A(A)h(A)=0.
\]
We now have \(\sum_iE_i=I\) and \(E_iE_j=0\) whenever \(i\neq j\). By Lemma 6.16,
\[
V=U_1\oplus\cdots\oplus U_m,
\]
and the \(E_i\) are the projections onto these subspaces.

\textbf{Step 3: Show \(V_{\lambda_i}\subseteq U_i\).}
Let \(x\in V_{\lambda_i}\), meaning \(Ax=\lambda_i x\). For every polynomial \(f\), we have \(f(A)x=f(\lambda_i)x\) (Lemma 6.9). By construction, \(p_i(\lambda_i)=1\), so
\[
E_ix=p_i(A)x=p_i(\lambda_i)x=x.
\]
Since \(x\) is an output of \(E_i\), it belongs to its image \(R(E_i)=U_i\). Hence \(V_{\lambda_i}\subseteq U_i\).

\textbf{Step 4: Show \(U_i\subseteq V_{\lambda_i}\).}
Multiplying the definition of \(p_i\) by the one factor it omits gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
(X-\lambda_i)p_i(X)
&=(X-\lambda_i)\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{X-\lambda_j}{\lambda_i-\lambda_j}\\
&=\frac{\prod_{k=1}^m(X-\lambda_k)}
{\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)}
=\frac{p_A(X)}{\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The denominator is a nonzero scalar because the eigenvalues are distinct. Substitute \(A\) for \(X\) and use \(p_A(A)=0\):
\[
(A-\lambda_iI)E_i
=(A-\lambda_iI)p_i(A)
=\frac{p_A(A)}{\prod_{j\neq i}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)}
=0.
\]
Take any \(u\in U_i=R(E_i)\). By the definition of image, \(u=E_iv\) for some \(v\in V\). Therefore
\[
(A-\lambda_iI)u=(A-\lambda_iI)E_iv=0,
\]
which is equivalent to \(Au=\lambda_i u\). Thus \(u\in N(A-\lambda_iI)=V_{\lambda_i}\), proving \(U_i\subseteq V_{\lambda_i}\).

Combining the inclusions gives \(U_i=V_{\lambda_i}\) for every \(i\). Replacing \(U_i\) in the direct sum from Step 2, we conclude
\[
\boxed{V=V_{\lambda_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\lambda_m}},
\]
as required.

\item \textbf{(ii) \(\Rightarrow\) diagonalizable.}
Choose a basis of each eigenspace \(V_{\lambda_i}\).
Because their sum is direct and equals \(V\), the union of these
bases is a basis of \(V\) consisting of eigenvectors. Hence \(A\)
is diagonalizable.
\end{proofsteps}

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why does \(q(A)x=0\) for eigenvectors imply \(q(A)=0\)?}
The \(x\) does not disappear: diagonalizability provides an eigenvector
basis \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\). For \emph{any} \(v=\sum_k a_kx_k\),
\[
q(A)v=\sum_k a_kq(A)x_k=\sum_k a_k\,0=0.
\]
Thus \(q(A)\) sends \emph{every} vector to zero, which is precisely
the statement that \(q(A)\) is the zero map.
\end{personalnote}

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why does \(p_A=q\) in (1)?}
We know \(p_A\mid q\) by Lemma 6.11. Every \(\lambda_i\) is a root
of \(p_A\) by Lemma 6.12; because these roots are distinct,
their product \(q\) also divides \(p_A\). Two monic polynomials
dividing one another are equal. \emph{Monic matters} to rule out
a nonunit scalar multiple.
\end{personalnote}

\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
\textbf{Lagrange polynomials:} \(p_i(\lambda_j)=\delta_{ij}\):
they equal \(1\) at their own eigenvalue and \(0\) at every other one.
\textbf{Range:} \(R(E_i)=\{E_iv:v\in V\}\).
\textbf{Projection:} for a direct sum, \(E_i\) extracts the \(i\)-th
component of a vector.
\textbf{Direct sum:} each vector has a \emph{unique} expression
as a sum of vectors from the stated subspaces.
\end{personalnote}

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why do the Lagrange polynomials sum to \(1\)?}
Each \(p_i\) has degree at most \(m-1\), and
\(\sum_i p_i(\lambda_k)=1\) for all \(m\) distinct eigenvalues.
Thus \(\sum_i p_i(X)-1\) has degree \(<m\) and \(m\) roots,
so it is identically zero (Lemma 6.17).

\textbf{Why does Lemma 6.16 apply?} The maps \(E_i\) sum to \(I\)
and their pairwise products vanish. These are exactly its hypotheses:
they give the direct-sum decomposition by the ranges \(R(E_i)\).
The final two inclusions identify these ranges as the eigenspaces.
\end{personalnote}

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Minimal versus characteristic polynomial.}
The condition concerns \(p_A\), \emph{not} the characteristic polynomial.
For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,2,3)\),
\[
\chi_A(X)=(X-2)^2(X-3),\qquad
p_A(X)=(X-2)(X-3).
\]
This matrix is already diagonal, even though its characteristic polynomial
has a repeated root. Repeated roots of the \emph{minimal} polynomial,
however, prevent diagonalizability.
\end{personalnote}

\part*{Topic B: Diagonalization of Real Symmetric Bilinear Forms and Sylvester's Law of Inertia}

\section*{Definitions for understanding}
Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field \(\F\) of characteristic different from \(2\).
A \emph{bilinear form} is a map \(B:V\times V\to\F\) linear in each argument separately.
It is \emph{symmetric} if \(B(u,v)=B(v,u)\) for all \(u,v\).
Vectors \(u,v\) are \emph{orthogonal with respect to \(B\)} when \(B(u,v)=0\).
A basis \((x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) is \emph{orthogonal} if \(B(x_i,x_j)=0\) for \(i\neq j\).
An \emph{alternating} form satisfies \(B(v,v)=0\) for all \(v\).
The \emph{radical} is
\[
V^\perp=\{v\in V:B(v,w)=0\text{ for every }w\in V\}.
\]
These definitions concern \(B\), not necessarily the usual Euclidean dot product.

\section{Theorem 4.11: Orthogonal bases for symmetric forms}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(B\in\operatorname{Sym}(V)\), there is an orthogonal basis of \(V\); equivalently, the matrix of \(B\) in that basis is diagonal.

\textbf{Proof (induction on \(n=\dim V\)).}
If \(n=0\), the empty basis works; if \(B=0\), every basis is orthogonal.
Assume \(n\geq1\) and \(B\neq0\). By Lemmas 4.7 and 4.9, a nonzero symmetric form in characteristic different from \(2\) is not alternating. Choose \(x\in V\) with \(B(x,x)\neq0\).
Set
\[
f:V\to\F,\quad f(v)=B(x,v),\qquad
H=\ker f=\{v\in V:B(x,v)=0\}.
\]
Since \(f(x)\neq0\), the image of \(f\) is the one-dimensional space \(\F\). Rank-nullity gives \(\dim H=n-1\).
The restriction \(B|_{H\times H}\) is symmetric, so by the induction hypothesis \(H\) has an orthogonal basis \(h_1,\ldots,h_{n-1}\).
Since \(B(x,x)\neq0\), we have \(x\notin H\); thus
\((x,h_1,\ldots,h_{n-1})\) is a linearly independent set of \(n\) vectors and hence a basis of \(V\). It is orthogonal since \(B(x,h_i)=0\) and the \(h_i\) are mutually orthogonal. \(\square\)

\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
\textbf{Empty basis:} the zero-dimensional vector space is \(\{0\}\), and its basis is the empty set; it spans \(\{0\}\) and is linearly independent.

\textbf{Why can we choose \(x\) with \(B(x,x)\neq0\)?}
An alternating form has \(B(v,v)=0\) for every \(v\).
If a symmetric \(B\) satisfied this, then
\(0=B(u+v,u+v)=2B(u,v)\) for every \(u,v\);
since \(\operatorname{char}\F\neq2\), this would imply \(B=0\), a contradiction.
\end{personalnote}
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is \(\dim H=n-1\)?}
The map \(f(v)=B(x,v)\) is linear, and \(H=\ker f\).
Because \(f(x)=B(x,x)\neq0\), its image is a nonzero subspace of \(\F\).
But \(\F\), viewed as a vector space over itself, has dimension \(1\);
therefore \(\dim\operatorname{Im}f=1\). Rank-nullity now gives
\[
n=\dim V=\dim\ker f+\dim\operatorname{Im}f=\dim H+1.
\]
\textbf{What makes this induction?}
We assume the theorem is true for \emph{every} symmetric bilinear form
on a space of dimension \(n-1\), apply it to the smaller space \(H\),
and then add the one missing direction \(x\) to obtain a basis of \(V\).
The cases \(n=0\) (and \(B=0\)) start the argument.
\end{personalnote}

\section{Theorem 4.15: Sylvester's law of inertia}
\textbf{Statement.} Let \(V\) be real, \(n=\dim V\), and \(m=n-\dim V^\perp\).
There exists an ordered basis \((x_1,\ldots,x_n)\) and a \emph{unique}
integer \(0\leq k\leq m\) such that
\[
B(x_i,x_j)=
\begin{cases}
1 & 1\leq i=j\leq k,\\
-1 & k<i=j\leq m,\\
0 & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus the matrix is \(\operatorname{diag}(I_k,-I_{m-k},0_{n-m})\).

\textbf{Proof of existence.}
By Theorem 4.11, choose an orthogonal basis \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\).
Write \(a_i=B(x_i,x_i)\); all off-diagonal entries vanish.
For each \(a_i\neq0\), replace \(x_i\) by \(y_i=x_i/\sqrt{|a_i|}\).
By bilinearity,
\[
B(y_i,y_i)=\frac{B(x_i,x_i)}{|a_i|}
=\frac{a_i}{|a_i|}=
\begin{cases}1&a_i>0,\\-1&a_i<0.\end{cases}
\]
For \(a_i=0\), keep \(y_i=x_i\). Rescaling preserves orthogonality.
Reorder the basis so positive entries precede negative entries and zeros.
The vectors corresponding to zero diagonal entries span \(V^\perp\):
each is orthogonal to the entire basis, and conversely a vector in
\(V^\perp\) cannot have a nonzero coefficient in a direction with
diagonal entry \(+1\) or \(-1\). Hence there are exactly
\(\dim V^\perp=n-m\) zeros, giving the required form.

\textbf{Proof of uniqueness.}
A symmetric bilinear form is \emph{positive definite} on a subspace
\(W\) if \(B(w,w)>0\) for every \(0\neq w\in W\).
We show that \(k\) is the maximal dimension of such a subspace.
Indeed, \(W_0=\operatorname{Span}(x_1,\ldots,x_k)\) has dimension \(k\)
and \(B(w,w)=a_1^2+\cdots+a_k^2>0\) for
\(0\neq w=\sum_{i=1}^k a_ix_i\).
Now let \(W\subseteq V\) be any subspace on which \(B\) is positive definite,
and set \(U=\operatorname{Span}(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_n)\).
Then \(\dim U=n-k\), while \(B(u,u)\leq0\) for every \(u\in U\).
Thus \(U\cap W=\{0\}\), since a nonzero vector in the intersection
would have both \(B(v,v)\leq0\) and \(B(v,v)>0\).
Consequently,
\[
(n-k)+\dim W=\dim U+\dim W
=\dim(U+W)\leq\dim V=n,
\]
so \(\dim W\leq k\).
Therefore \(k\) is the maximum dimension of a positive-definite
subspace, an intrinsic property of \(B\), and is unique. \(\square\)

\begin{personalnote}[title=Definitions for understanding]
\textbf{Positive definite (Definition 4.16):}
\(B(w,w)>0\) for all nonzero \(w\) in the relevant space.
\textbf{The radical \(V^\perp\):} vectors orthogonal to \emph{every}
vector in \(V\). The number of zeros in the normalized diagonal
matrix is \(n-m=\dim V^\perp\); the number of negatives is \(m-k\).
\textbf{What does uniqueness claim?} Existence gives \emph{some} basis
with \(+1,-1,0\). Uniqueness says every such normalized basis has
the same number \(k\) of positive entries, not that the basis itself
is unique.
\end{personalnote}
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is \(\dim U=n-k\)?}
The vectors \(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_n\) are \(n-k\) members of a basis,
so they are independent and form a basis of \(U\).
They correspond precisely to the negative and zero diagonal entries.

\textbf{Why does \(U\cap W=\{0\}\) give \(\dim W\leq k\)?}
The intersection being zero means \(U+W\) is a direct sum, so
\(\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W\).
Since \(U+W\subseteq V\), its dimension cannot exceed \(n\):
\[
(n-k)+\dim W=\dim(U+W)\leq n
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\dim W\leq k.
\]
Because the \emph{maximum dimension} of a positive-definite
subspace is defined without choosing a basis, \(k\) is independent
of the chosen normalized basis.
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Definitions for understanding: Quadrics}
A \emph{quadratic form} is \(q(x)=B(x,x)\) for a bilinear form \(B\).
A \emph{quadric} in \(\mathbb R^n\) is the solution set of
\(q(x)+y(x)=c\), where \(y\) is a linear form and \(c\in\mathbb R\).
Two quadrics \(X_1,X_2\) are \emph{equivalent} if an invertible affine map
\(T(x)=Ax+b\), \(A\in GL_n(\mathbb R)\), sends \(X_1\) onto \(X_2\).
In simple terms, we may make an invertible linear change of coordinates
and translate the origin; the equation changes, but the resulting sets
represent the same affine geometry.

\section{Theorem 4.20: Classification of quadrics - optional}
\textbf{Statement.} Every quadric in \(\mathbb R^n\) is equivalent to
one given by
\[
x_1^2+\cdots+x_k^2-x_{k+1}^2-\cdots-x_m^2=1\ \text{or}\ 0,
\qquad 0\leq k\leq m\leq n, \tag{4.11}
\]
or by
\[
x_1^2+\cdots+x_k^2-x_{k+1}^2-\cdots-x_m^2=x_{m+1},
\qquad 0\leq k\leq m<n. \tag{4.12}
\]

\textbf{Proof (as sketched in the lecture notes).}
Start with \(q(x)+y(x)=c\).
By Sylvester's law (Theorem 4.15), an invertible linear change of
coordinates makes the quadratic part
\[
q(x)=x_1^2+\cdots+x_k^2-x_{k+1}^2-\cdots-x_m^2.
\]
Write \(y(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ix_i\).
For \(1\leq i\leq m\), complete the square and translate \(x_i\)
to eliminate the linear term \(a_ix_i\).
For instance,
\[
x_1^2+\gamma x_1=(x_1+\gamma/2)^2-\gamma^2/4.
\]
(The same argument works for a negative squared term.)
After absorbing the resulting constants, the equation has the form
\[
q(x)+a_{m+1}x_{m+1}+\cdots+a_nx_n=c'.
\]
If all remaining linear coefficients vanish, we have \(q(x)=c'\).
If \(c'=0\), this is (4.11). If \(c'\neq0\), rescale coordinates
to normalize \(|c'|\) to \(1\); if \(c'<0\), multiply the equation
by \(-1\) and exchange the positive and negative groups.
This gives (4.11) with right-hand side \(1\).

Otherwise some coefficient \(a_i\) for \(i>m\) is nonzero, so \(m<n\).
An invertible linear change among the last \(n-m\) coordinates
combines their linear terms into one coordinate \(x_{m+1}\).
A translation in that coordinate absorbs \(c'\), and a sign change
if needed yields \(q(x)=x_{m+1}\), which is (4.12). \(\square\)

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why only complete the square for \(i\leq m\)?}
Only those coordinates have nonzero squared terms.
The remaining coordinates \(x_{m+1},\ldots,x_n\) have no quadratic terms;
a nonzero linear combination of them instead becomes the coordinate
on the right of (4.12).

so both invertible changes of basis and shifts of origin are allowed.

\part*{Topic E: Orthogonal Diagonalization of Normal and Symmetric Maps}
\section*{Definitions for understanding}
An \emph{inner product space} is a vector space over \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\) equipped with a inner product \(\langle x,y\rangle\). Our convention is linearity in the first variable and conjugate-linearity in the second:
\(\langle ax,by\rangle=a\overline b\langle x,y\rangle\).

Definition 7.1 (Inner product). An inner product is a map \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb F\) such that (a) \(x\mapsto\langle x,y\rangle\) is linear for every fixed \(y\); (b) \(\langle x,y\rangle=\overline{\langle y,x\rangle}\); and (c) \(\langle x,x\rangle>0\) for every nonzero \(x\). In particular, (b) makes \(\langle x,x\rangle\) real, and (a)--(b) imply conjugate-linearity in the second argument.

An \emph{orthonormal basis} \((e_1,\ldots,e_n)\) spans \(V\) and satisfies \(\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}\): distinct basis vectors are orthogonal and each has norm \(1\).

The \emph{adjoint} \(A^*\) is characterized by
\(\langle Ax,y\rangle{=}\langle x,A^*y\rangle\).


A map is \emph{normal} if \(AA^*=A^*A\), and \emph{self-adjoint} if \(A=A^*\). Over \(\mathbb R\), self-adjoint maps are called \emph{symmetric}.


The \emph{minimal polynomial} \(p_A\) is the unique monic polynomial of least degree with \(p_A(A)=0\).


The eigenspace \(V_\lambda(A)=N(A-\lambda I)\), and \(\sigma(A)\) is the set of eigenvalues.

\section{Lemma 7.22: Properties of normal maps}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\) is normal, then
\[
\begin{array}{ll}
\text{(i)}&N(A^*)=N(A),\\
\text{(ii)}&N(A^m)=N(A)\quad(m\geq1),\\
\text{(iii)}&V_\lambda(A)=V_{\overline\lambda}(A^*),\\
\text{(iv)}&V_\lambda(A)\perp V_\mu(A)\quad(\lambda\neq\mu).
\end{array}
\]

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{In words, (iii).} The $\lambda$-eigenspace of $A$ is equal to the $\Bar{\lambda}$-eigenspace of $A^*$ for all $\lambda$.

\medskip
\textbf{In words, (iv).} The eigenspaces of A are mutually orthogonal. This means that for any distinct eigenvalues \(\lambda\neq\mu\), every \(x\in V_\lambda(A)\) and \(y\in V_\mu(A)\) satisfy \(\langle x,y\rangle=0\).
\end{personalnote}
\textbf{Proof.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item[(i)] By Lemma 7.16(ii), \(N(A)=N(A^*A)\) and \(N(A^*)=N(AA^*)\).
Normality gives \(A^*A=AA^*\), hence \(N(A)=N(A^*)\).
\item[(ii)] For \(m\geq2\), if \(A^mx=0\), then
\[
A(A^{m-1}x)=0
\ \underset{\text{(i)}}{\Longrightarrow}\
A^*A(A^{m-2}x)=0
\ \underset{\text{Lemma 7.16(ii)}:\ N(A)=N(A^*A)}{\Longrightarrow}\
A^{m-1}x=0.
\]
The reverse implication is automatic. Thus \(N(A^m)=N(A^{m-1})\), and iteration gives \(N(A^m)=N(A)\).
\item[(iii)] Since \(A-\lambda I\) is normal, (i) gives
\[
V_\lambda(A)=N(A-\lambda I)
=N((A-\lambda I)^*)
=N(A^*-\overline\lambda I)
=V_{\overline\lambda}(A^*).
\]
\item[(iv)] Let \(Ax=\mu x\), \(Ay=\lambda y\), where \(\mu\neq\lambda\).
By (iii), \(Ay=\lambda y\) means \(y\in V_\lambda(A)=V_{\overline\lambda}(A^*)\), hence \(A^*y=\overline\lambda y\). Thus
\[
\mu\langle x,y\rangle
=\langle Ax,y\rangle
=\langle x,A^*y\rangle
=\langle x,\overline\lambda y\rangle
=\lambda\langle x,y\rangle.
\]
Hence \((\mu-\lambda)\langle x,y\rangle=0\), so \(\langle x,y\rangle=0\).
\end{proofsteps}

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is \(A-\lambda I\) normal?}
Its adjoint is \(A^*-\overline\lambda I\). Expanding the two products
\((A-\lambda I)(A^*-\overline\lambda I)\) and
\((A^*-\overline\lambda I)(A-\lambda I)\) gives identical cross terms; their difference is \(AA^*-A^*A=0\).

\medskip

\textbf{Why does the scalar become conjugated?}
The adjoint is conjugate-linear in scalars: \((\lambda I)^*=\overline\lambda I\), as follows from the defining inner-product identity.

\medskip
\textbf{Why choose \(Ax=\mu x\) and \(Ay=\lambda y\)?}
By definition \(V_\mu(A)=\{x:Ax=\mu x\}\) and \(V_\lambda(A)=\{y:Ay=\lambda y\}\). So we have chosen arbitrary vectors from the two eigenspaces. To show that the eigenspaces are orthogonal, we must show \(\langle x,y\rangle=0\) for every such pair. The computation in (iv) proves exactly this when \(\mu\neq\lambda\).
\end{personalnote}

(i) & \(N(A)=N(A^*A)=N(AA^*)=N(A^*)\).\\
(ii) & Use (i) and Lemma 7.16(ii) to lower the exponent one step.\\
(iii) & Apply (i) to \(A-\lambda I\).\\
(iv) & Apply the adjoint identity and (iii) to obtain \((\mu-\lambda)\langle x,y\rangle=0\).\\\hline
\end{tabularx}}

\section{Lemma 7.23: The minimal polynomial has no repeated roots}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(A\) is normal, its minimal polynomial \(p_A\) has no repeated roots.

\textbf{Proof.} Let \(m\geq1\), and assume \(p_A(X)=(X-\lambda)^m q(X)\), where \(q\in\mathbb F[X]\). Then \(q(A)\) maps into the null space of \((A-\lambda I)^m\). Since \(A-\lambda I\) is normal, Lemma 7.22(ii) gives
\[
N((A-\lambda I)^m)=N(A-\lambda I).
\]
Hence \((A-\lambda I)q(A)=0\), and \(m=1\) by minimality of \(p_A\). \(\square\)

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why does \(q(A)\) map into this null space?}
For every \(x\in V\), \(0=p_A(A)x=(A-\lambda I)^m q(A)x\). Therefore \(q(A)x\in N((A-\lambda I)^m)\). The null-space equality shows that \((A-\lambda I)q(A)x=0\) for every \(x\).

\medskip
\textbf{Why must \(m=1\)?}
The polynomial \(r(X)=(X-\lambda)q(X)\) now annihilates \(A\). If \(m>1\), then \(\deg r=1+\deg q<m+\deg q=\deg p_A\), contradicting the minimal degree of the nonzero annihilating polynomial \(p_A\). Thus \(m=1\).
\end{personalnote}

\section*{Definitions for understanding: Spectral resolution}
A map is \emph{orthogonally diagonalizable} if \(V\) has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, equivalently
\(V=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}V_\lambda\) as an \emph{orthogonal} direct sum.

For each eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the orthogonal projection \(E_\lambda\) sends a vector to its component in \(V_\lambda\).
A component \(v_\lambda\) is the particular vector in \(V_\lambda\) appearing in the unique sum \(v=\sum_\lambda v_\lambda\). If \(v=\sum_\lambda v_\lambda\) with \(v_\lambda\in V_\lambda\), then
\(E_\lambda v=v_\lambda\). Consequently,
\[
E_\lambda^*=E_\lambda,\quad
E_\lambda E_\mu=\begin{cases}E_\lambda&\lambda=\mu,\\0&\lambda\neq\mu,\end{cases}
\quad \sum_\lambda E_\lambda=I.
\]
The \emph{spectral resolution} is
\[
A=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\lambda E_\lambda.
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Intuition for orthogonal projection.}
For example, suppose the two eigenspaces in \(\mathbb R^2\) are the coordinate axes. We can write \(v=(3,5)=(3,0)+(0,5)\), where each summand lies in one eigenspace. The projection \(E_{\lambda_1}\) onto the first eigenspace gives \(E_{\lambda_1}v=(3,0)\), and \(E_{\lambda_2}v=(0,5)\). Once we have \((3,0)\), projecting onto the first axis again leaves it unchanged: \(E_{\lambda_1}^2v=E_{\lambda_1}v\). Projecting \((3,0)\) onto the second axis instead gives zero: \(E_{\lambda_2}E_{\lambda_1}v=0\). The same reasoning works for any orthogonal direct sum of eigenspaces.
\end{personalnote}

\section{Theorem 7.25: Spectral theorem for normal maps over \(\mathbb C\)}
\textbf{Statement.} Let \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\), where \(\dim V<\infty\) and \(\mathbb F=\mathbb C\).

(a) \(A\) is normal $\Longleftrightarrow$ it is orthogonally diagonalizable;

(b) \(A\) is self-adjoint $\Longleftrightarrow$ it is normal and \(\sigma(A)\subseteq\mathbb R\).

\textbf{Proof of (a).}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item Suppose \(A\) is orthogonally diagonalizable. The spectral resolution gives
\[
A=\sum_\lambda\lambda E_\lambda,\qquad
A^*=\sum_\lambda\overline\lambda E_\lambda
\]
since \(E_\lambda^*=E_\lambda\).
By the projection identities,
\[
AA^*=\sum_{\lambda,\mu}\lambda\overline\mu E_\lambda E_\mu
=\sum_\lambda|\lambda|^2E_\lambda=A^*A.
\]
Thus \(A\) is normal.
\item Conversely, assume \(A\) is normal. Over \(\mathbb C\), the fundamental theorem of algebra implies \(p_A\) splits; Lemma 7.23 says its roots are distinct. Theorem 6.18 therefore implies \(A\) is diagonalizable:
\(V=\bigoplus_\lambda V_\lambda\).
Hence \(A\) is diagonalizable by Theorem 6.18, and by Lemma 7.22(iv) it is orthogonally diagonalizable. In particular, the spectral resolution holds with self-adjoint projections.
\end{proofsteps}
\textbf{Proof of (b).}
If \(A=A^*\), then \(A\) is normal. For \(Ax=\lambda x\) with \(x\neq0\), Lemma 7.22(iii) gives \(A^*x=\overline\lambda x\), hence
\(\lambda x=\overline\lambda x\) and \(\lambda\in\mathbb R\).

Conversely, if \(A\) is normal with real spectrum, (a) gives
\[
A=\sum_\lambda\lambda E_\lambda,\qquad
A^*=\sum_\lambda\overline\lambda E_\lambda
=\sum_\lambda\lambda E_\lambda=A.
\]
Thus \(A\) is self-adjoint. \(\square\)

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is the combined basis orthonormal?}
Diagonalizability first guarantees that the eigenspaces \emph{span all of \(V\)}. Orthogonality alone would not guarantee spanning. Within each eigenspace choose an orthonormal basis; vectors from \emph{different} eigenspaces are perpendicular by Lemma 7.22(iv). Thus the union both spans \(V\) and is orthonormal.

\medskip

\textbf{Why is \(AA^*=A^*A\)?}
The product \(E_\lambda E_\mu\) vanishes for distinct eigenvalues and equals \(E_\lambda\) when they coincide, so both products reduce to \(\sum_\lambda|\lambda|^2E_\lambda\).
\end{personalnote}

\section{Lemma 7.26: Injectivity of \(A^2+\lambda I\)}
\textbf{Statement.} Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional real inner product space and \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\) symmetric. Then \(A^2+\lambda I\) is injective for every \(\lambda>0\).

\textbf{Proof.} Suppose \((A^2+\lambda I)x=0\).
Since \(\lambda>0\), positivity of the inner product and symmetry of \(A\) give
\[
0\leq\lambda\langle x,x\rangle
\leq\langle Ax,Ax\rangle+\lambda\langle x,x\rangle
=\langle(A^2+\lambda I)x,x\rangle=0.
\]
Hence \(\lambda\langle x,x\rangle=0\), so \(\langle x,x\rangle=0\) and \(x=0\).
Thus \(N(A^2+\lambda I)=\{0\}\), proving injectivity. \(\square\)

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{What do the symbols mean?}

Symmetric means \(\langle Au,v\rangle=\langle u,Av\rangle\).

Injective means the only vector sent to zero is zero.

The map \(A^2+\lambda I\) sends \(x\) to \(A(Ax)+\lambda x\); \(I\) is the identity.

The equality
\(\langle A^2x,x\rangle=\langle Ax,Ax\rangle\)
uses symmetry with \(u=Ax,v=x\).
Both \(\langle Ax,Ax\rangle\) and \(\lambda\langle x,x\rangle\) are nonnegative, so if their sum is zero, each is zero.
\end{personalnote}

\section{Lemma 7.27: Real splitting of the minimal polynomial}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(A\) is symmetric over \(\mathbb R\), then its minimal polynomial \(p_A\in\mathbb R[X]\) splits over \(\mathbb R\).

\textbf{Proof.} Suppose \(p_A\) has a non-real root \(\lambda=\alpha+i\beta\), \(\beta\neq0\).
Since \(p_A\) has real coefficients, \(\overline\lambda=\alpha-i\beta\) is also a root. Thus
\[
p_A(X)=(X-\lambda)(X-\overline\lambda)q(X)
=\big((X-\alpha)^2+\beta^2\big)q(X)
\]
with \(q\in\mathbb R[X]\). Evaluating at \(A\) yields
\[
\big((A-\alpha I)^2+\beta^2I\big)q(A)=0.
\]
The map \(B=A-\alpha I\) is symmetric, and \(\beta^2>0\).
By Lemma 7.26, \(B^2+\beta^2I\) is injective. Hence \(q(A)x=0\) for every \(x\in V\), so \(q(A)=0\).
But \(\deg q=\deg p_A-2<\deg p_A\), contradicting the minimality of \(p_A\). Therefore no non-real root exists; by the fundamental theorem of algebra, all factors are real linear factors. \(\square\)

\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is the conjugate also a root?}
For a polynomial with real coefficients,
\(p_A(\overline z)=\overline{p_A(z)}\). Thus \(p_A(\lambda)=0\) implies \(p_A(\overline\lambda)=0\).

\medskip

\textbf{Why does the image lie in a null space?}
For each \(x\), the displayed operator identity says
\(((A-\alpha I)^2+\beta^2I)(q(A)x)=0\).
Thus \(q(A)x\) belongs to the kernel of the first factor. Since that factor is injective, its kernel is \(\{0\}\), so \(q(A)x=0\) for every \(x\).

\medskip

\textbf{Why does the degree decrease by two?}
The factor \((X-\alpha)^2+\beta^2\) has degree \(2\), so \(\deg q=\deg p_A-2\).
This is a contradiction because \(q\) is a nonzero polynomial annihilating \(A\) of smaller degree.
\end{personalnote}

\section{Theorem 7.28: Spectral theorem for symmetric maps over \(\mathbb R\)}
\textbf{Statement.} For \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\) on a finite-dimensional real inner product space,
\[
\boxed{A\text{ is orthogonally diagonalizable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A=A^*.}
\]
\textbf{Proof.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item Assume \(A=A^*\). Then \(A\) is normal, because
\(AA^*=A^2=A^*A\).
By Lemma 7.27, \(p_A\) splits over \(\mathbb R\), and by Lemma 7.23 its roots are distinct.
Theorem 6.18 implies \(A\) is diagonalizable:
\(V=\bigoplus_\lambda V_\lambda\).
By Lemma 7.22(iv), distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal.
Choose an orthonormal basis of each eigenspace using Gram--Schmidt; together these form an orthonormal eigenvector basis of \(V\).
\item Conversely, if \(A\) is orthogonally diagonalizable, then
\(A=\sum_\lambda\lambda E_\lambda\), where \(E_\lambda^*=E_\lambda\).
Since the field is \(\mathbb R\), each \(\lambda\) is real, and
\[
A^*=\sum_\lambda\overline\lambda E_\lambda
=\sum_\lambda\lambda E_\lambda=A.
\]
Thus \(A\) is symmetric.
\end{proofsteps}
\begin{personalnote}

\textbf{Why can we choose bases of each eigenspace?}
Each eigenspace is a finite-dimensional inner product subspace, so Gram--Schmidt gives an orthonormal basis. More precisely, starting from any basis of an eigenspace, the Gram--Schmidt procedure produces orthonormal vectors spanning that same eigenspace. Because these new vectors are still in the eigenspace, they remain eigenvectors. Theorem 6.18 first guarantees that the eigenspaces sum to all of \(V\); Lemma 7.22(iv) guarantees vectors chosen from distinct eigenspaces are orthogonal.
\end{personalnote}

Orthogonally diagonalizable \(\Rightarrow\) symmetric & Spectral resolution and \(E_\lambda^*=E_\lambda\), with real eigenvalues.\\\hline

\section*{Big Picture: Topic E}
For normal maps over \(\mathbb C\), the fundamental theorem of algebra gives splitting and Lemma 7.23 gives distinct roots; Theorem 6.18 gives diagonalizability, and Lemma 7.22(iv) upgrades it to \emph{orthogonal} diagonalizability (Theorem 7.25).
For symmetric maps over \(\mathbb R\), splitting is not automatic: Lemma 7.26 supplies an injectivity argument, and Lemma 7.27 uses it to rule out non-real roots. Lemma 7.23 and Theorem 6.18 then give diagonalizability; Lemma 7.22(iv) again gives an orthonormal eigenbasis (Theorem 7.28).
\[
\boxed{\begin{gathered}

\mathbb R:\ \text{symmetric}\Longleftrightarrow\text{orthogonally diagonalizable}.
\end{gathered}}
\]

\part*{Topic I: Hahn--Banach}

\section*{Definitions for understanding}
Let \(U\subseteq V\) be a subspace of a normed vector space over \(\F\).
A linear functional \(z:U\to\F\) is \emph{bounded} when \(|z(u)|\leq M\|u\|\) for some \(M\geq0\) and every \(u\in U\).
The continuous dual \(U^*\) consists of these bounded functionals, while \(U'\) consists of all linear functionals. The norm is
\[
 \|z\|=\sup_{\|u\|\leq1}|z(u)|.
\]
An extension \(y\) of \(z\) satisfies \(y|_U=z\): it agrees with \(z\) on all of \(U\), but has a larger domain.

\section{Theorem 11.13: Hahn--Banach theorem}
\textbf{Statement.} Let \(U\subseteq V\) be a subspace and \(z\in U^*\). There exists \(y\in V^*\) such that
\[
 \boxed{y|_U=z,\qquad\|y\|=\|z\|.}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Big idea.} A bounded functional defined on only a subspace can be extended to the whole space without increasing its norm. We first reduce the complex case to the real case (Lemma 11.14); then extend one dimension (Lemma 11.15); finally use Zorn's lemma to obtain the full extension.
\end{personalnote}

\section{Lemma 11.14: Passing between complex and real functionals}
\textbf{Statement.} For a normed complex space \(V\), let \(V_{\mathbb R}\) be the same set of vectors and the same norm, but with scalar multiplication restricted to \(\mathbb R\).
The map \(z\mapsto x=\operatorname{Re}z\) is a norm-preserving bijection
\(V'\to(V_{\mathbb R})'\).

\textbf{Proof, following the lecture notes.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \textbf{Real-linearity.} If \(z\in V'\), define \(x(v)=\operatorname{Re}z(v)\). For real \(a,b\),
\[
 x(av+bw)=\operatorname{Re}(az(v)+bz(w))=ax(v)+bx(w).
\]
Hence \(x\in(V_{\mathbb R})'\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{What is \(V_{\mathbb R}\)?} We have not changed the vectors at all. We are simply viewing the complex vector space as a real one, so only real-linear combinations are required. Taking real parts is real-linear, but not generally complex-linear.
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Formula (11.5).} The notes give
\[
 \boxed{z(v)=x(v)-ix(iv).}\tag{11.5}
\]
Indeed, write \(z(v)=a+ib\), with \(a,b\in\mathbb R\). Then \(x(v)=a\), while
\[
 z(iv)=iz(v)=i(a+ib)=-b+ia\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(iv)=-b.
\]
Thus \(x(v)-ix(iv)=a-i(-b)=a+ib=z(v)\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why does this prove injectivity?} Two complex functionals with the same real part \(x\) are both recovered by this formula, so they are equal. Although \(x\) is real-valued, evaluating it at \(iv\) recovers the imaginary component.
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Conversely.} Given any real-linear \(x\in(V_{\mathbb R})'\), define \(z(v)=x(v)-ix(iv)\). This is real-linear, and
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 z(iv)&=x(iv)-ix(-v)=x(iv)+ix(v)\\
 &=i\bigl(x(v)-ix(iv)\bigr)=iz(v).
 \end{aligned}
\]
Since each complex scalar is \(a+ib\) with \(a,b\in\mathbb R\), real-linearity together with \(z(iv)=iz(v)\) proves complex-linearity. Also \(\operatorname{Re}z=x\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why does this prove surjectivity?} We started with an arbitrary \(x\) in the target space and constructed a complex-linear \(z\) whose real part equals exactly that \(x\).
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Norm preservation.} Let \(B=\{v:\|v\|\leq1\}\). For \(|\alpha|\leq1\),
\[
 \|\alpha v\|=|\alpha|\|v\|\leq1\quad(v\in B).
\]
Hence the unit ball is stable under multiplication by complex scalars of modulus at most \(1\), as is its image \(z(B)\). This image is a disc centered at \(0\), possibly missing its boundary, of radius
\(\sup_{v\in B}|z(v)|=\|z\|\).
Taking real parts projects the disc onto an interval of the same radius; thus \(\|x\|=\|z\|\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is the radius unchanged?} The inequality
\(|x(v)|=|\operatorname{Re}z(v)|\leq|z(v)|\)
gives \(\|x\|\leq\|z\|\). Conversely, for \(z(v)\ne0\), choose
\(\alpha=\overline{z(v)}/|z(v)|\). Then \(|\alpha|=1\), \(\alpha v\in B\), and
\(x(\alpha v)=\operatorname{Re}(\alpha z(v))=|z(v)|\).
Take suprema to get \(\|z\|\leq\|x\|\).
\end{personalnote}
\end{proofsteps}

\section{Lemma 11.15: Extending one real dimension}
\textbf{Statement.} Theorem 11.13 holds when \(\F=\mathbb R\) and \(\operatorname{codim}U=1\).

\textbf{Proof, following the lecture notes.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \textbf{Normalize.} Without loss of generality \(\|z\|=1\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why can we assume this?} If \(\|z\|=c>0\), apply the norm-one case to \(\widetilde z=z/c\), extend it to \(\widetilde y\), and set \(y=c\widetilde y\). This satisfies \(y|_U=z\) and \(\|y\|=c\). If \(z=0\), extend by zero. Normalization simplifies the estimate to \(|z(u)|\leq\|u\|\).
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Choose a new direction.} Take \(v\in V\setminus U\). As \(\operatorname{codim}U=1\),
\[
 V=U\oplus\operatorname{Span}\{v\}.
\]
Every element has a unique representation \(u+\alpha v\): if \((\alpha-\beta)v\in U\), then \(\alpha=\beta\), since \(v\notin U\).

\item \textbf{Find compatible bounds.} For all \(u,u'\in U\),
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 z(u')-z(u)&=z(u'-u) &&\text{(linearity)}\\
 &\leq\|u'-u\| &&\text{(norm one)}\\
 &\leq\|u'+v\|+\|u+v\| &&\text{(triangle inequality).}
 \end{aligned}
\]
For the last inequality note \(u'-u=(u'+v)-(u+v)\).
Rearrange:
\[
 -z(u)-\|u+v\|\leq-z(u')+\|u'+v\|.
\]
It holds for \emph{every pair} \(u,u'\), so
\[
 \boxed{\sup_{u\in U}\bigl(-z(u)-\|u+v\|\bigr)
 \leq\inf_{u'\in U}\bigl(-z(u')+\|u'+v\|\bigr).}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why sup--inf?} We need one value \(\lambda=y(v)\) satisfying the norm bound for \emph{all} \(u\in U\). Every candidate lower bound is below every candidate upper bound, so there is a common interval. The bounds are finite: setting \(u'=0\) bounds the lower expressions above, while \(u=0\) bounds the upper expressions below.
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Choose \(\lambda\) from this interval.} We obtain for all \(u\in U\):
\[
 -z(u)-\|u+v\|\leq\lambda\leq-z(u)+\|u+v\|,
\]
which is equivalent to
\[
 \boxed{|z(u)+\lambda|\leq\|u+v\|.}
\]
The value of \(\lambda\) need not be unique; we only need one that works simultaneously for every \(u\).

\item \textbf{Define the extension.} The lecture notes define
\[
 \boxed{y(u+\alpha v)=z(u)+\alpha\lambda
 \quad(u\in U,\ \alpha\in\mathbb R).}
\]
Uniqueness of \(u+\alpha v\) makes \(y\) well-defined, and linearity of \(z\) makes \(y\) linear. Taking \(\alpha=0\) gives \(y|_U=z\).

\item \textbf{Verify the last calculation of the notes.} For \(\alpha\ne0\),
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 |y(u+\alpha v)|
 &=|\alpha|\left|z\left(\frac{u}{\alpha}\right)+\lambda\right|\\
 &\leq|\alpha|\left\|\frac{u}{\alpha}+v\right\|\\
 &=\|u+\alpha v\|.
 \end{aligned}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{First equality:} \(z(u)=\alpha z(u/\alpha)\) by linearity, so factor out \(\alpha\).
\textbf{Inequality:} Apply \(|z(w)+\lambda|\leq\|w+v\|\) from the previous step with \(w=u/\alpha\in U\).
\textbf{Last equality:} Homogeneity of the norm:
\(|\alpha|\|u/\alpha+v\|=\|\alpha(u/\alpha+v)\|\).
\end{personalnote}
For \(\alpha=0\), \(|y(u)|=|z(u)|\leq\|u\|\).
Hence \(|y(w)|\leq\|w\|\) for all \(w\in V\), so \(\|y\|\leq1\). Since \(y|_U=z\), restriction gives \(\|y\|\geq\|z\|=1\). Thus \(\boxed{\|y\|=\|z\|=1}\).
\end{proofsteps}

\end{tabularx}}

\section{Proof of Theorem 11.13: Zorn's lemma}
\textbf{Proof.} First let \(\F=\mathbb R\).
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \textbf{Collect all admissible extensions.} Let \(\mathcal X\) consist of pairs \((W,y)\) with \(U\subseteq W\subseteq V\), \(y\in W^*\), \(y|_U=z\), and \(\|y\|=\|z\|\).
It is nonempty: \((U,z)\in\mathcal X\).

\item \textbf{Order these pairs.} Set
\[
 (W_1,y_1)\leq(W_2,y_2)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 W_1\subseteq W_2\ \text{and}\ y_2|_{W_1}=y_1.
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why must the functionals agree?} A larger domain alone does not give an extension. The new functional must preserve all values already assigned on the smaller domain. This relation is a partial order; not every two pairs need be comparable.
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Upper bound for a totally ordered subset.} Take any chain \(\mathcal Y\subseteq\mathcal X\), and define
\[
 \widetilde W=\bigcup_{(W,y)\in\mathcal Y}W,\qquad
 \widetilde y(v)=y(v)\quad\text{whenever }v\in W,\ (W,y)\in\mathcal Y.
\]
\emph{Why is the union a subspace?} If \(a\in W_1\), \(b\in W_2\), total ordering says one of these domains contains the other. Then \(a,b\) both belong to a common subspace, which also contains all their linear combinations.

\emph{Why is \(\widetilde y\) well-defined?} If \(v\) lies in both \(W_1,W_2\), assume \((W_1,y_1)\leq(W_2,y_2)\); then \(y_2(v)=y_1(v)\). Linearity follows similarly. Moreover
\[
 |\widetilde y(v)|=|y(v)|\leq\|z\|\|v\|
\]
for every \(v\in\widetilde W\), so \(\|\widetilde y\|\leq\|z\|\).
Since \(\widetilde y|_U=z\), the reverse inequality holds, hence \(\|\widetilde y\|=\|z\|\).
Therefore \((\widetilde W,\widetilde y)\in\mathcal X\) and is an upper bound for the chain. For the empty chain, any member of \(\mathcal X\) is an upper bound.
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{What is totally ordered?} Any two elements \emph{within the chosen subset} can be compared. The full set \(\mathcal X\) need not be totally ordered. This condition is what makes the union a subspace and the glued functional unambiguous.
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Apply Zorn.} Every chain has an upper bound, so Zorn's lemma yields a maximal pair \((W,y)\in\mathcal X\).

\item \textbf{Show \(W=V\).} If \(W\subsetneq V\), choose \(v\notin W\) and set \(W'=W\oplus\operatorname{Span}\{v\}\). Then \(W\) has codimension one in \(W'\). Lemma 11.15 extends \(y\) to \(y'\in(W')^*\) with the same norm. Thus
\[
 (W,y)<(W',y')\in\mathcal X,
\]
contradicting maximality. So \(W=V\).
\end{proofsteps}
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is this the complete complex proof?} For complex \(z\), apply the real case to \(x=\operatorname{Re}z\) on \(U_{\mathbb R}\), obtaining an extension \(X\) to \(V_{\mathbb R}\). Set \(y(v)=X(v)-iX(iv)\). By Lemma 11.14 this is complex-linear, extends \(z\), and satisfies \(\|y\|=\|X\|=\|x\|=\|z\|\).
\end{personalnote}
\prooftable{\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.24\linewidth}X@{}}\hline
Step & Purpose\\\hline
Pairs and ordering & Collect compatible norm-preserving extensions.\\
Chains & Glue them together to obtain upper bounds.\\
Zorn & Obtain a maximal extension.\\
Lemma 11.15 & A proper domain could be enlarged, contradiction.\\\hline
\end{tabularx}}

\section{Corollary 11.16: Separating a point from a closed subspace}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(X\subseteq V\) is a closed subspace and \(v\notin X\), there exists \(y\in V^*\) with \(y|_X=0\) and \(y(v)\neq0\).

\textbf{Proof, following the lecture notes.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item Set
\[
 U=\operatorname{Span}\{v\}\oplus X,\qquad
 c=\inf_{x\in X}\|v-x\|.
\]
The sum is direct since \(v\notin X\); any \(\alpha v\in X\) with \(\alpha\ne0\) would imply \(v\in X\).
\item \textbf{Why \(c>0\)?} If \(c=0\), choose \(x_n\in X\) with \(\|v-x_n\|<1/n\). Then \(x_n\to v\), and because \(X\) is closed, \(v\in X\), a contradiction.
\begin{personalnote}
\(c\) is the distance of \(v\) from \(X\). We need \(c>0\) because the final functional will have value \(y(v)=c\neq0\). The infimum need not be attained.
\end{personalnote}
\item Define
\[
 \boxed{z(\alpha v+x)=c\alpha,\qquad \alpha\in\F,\ x\in X.}
\]
The direct sum makes the representation unique, so the definition is unambiguous and linear. We immediately have \(z(v)=c\) and \(z(x)=0\) for \(x\in X\).
\item For \(\alpha\neq0\) the lecture notes calculate
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 |z(\alpha v+x)|
 &=|\alpha|c\\
 &\leq|\alpha|\|v+\alpha^{-1}x\|\\
 &=\|\alpha v+x\|.
 \end{aligned}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why the middle inequality?} The definition of the infimum means \(c\leq\|v-x'\|\) for every \(x'\in X\). Choose \(x'=-\alpha^{-1}x\in X\). The final equality follows from homogeneity. If \(\alpha=0\), then \(|z(x)|=0\leq\|x\|\).
\end{personalnote}
Hence \(\|z\|\leq1\), so \(z\in U^*\).
\item Theorem 11.13 provides \(y\in V^*\) extending \(z\) with the same norm. Since \(v\in U\) and \(X\subseteq U\),
\[
 \boxed{y(v)=z(v)=c>0,\qquad y|_X=z|_X=0.}
\]
\end{proofsteps}
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Small notation correction.} At the point where the lecture notes prove boundedness, \(z\) is in \(U^*\), not yet in \(V^*\); the latter is the space containing the extension \(y\).

\textbf{Consequence.} With \(X=\{0\}\), every nonzero vector is detected by a bounded linear functional. For \(v_1\neq v_2\), apply the corollary to \(v=v_1-v_2\); then \(y(v_1)\neq y(v_2)\). Thus continuous linear functionals separate vectors.
\end{personalnote}

Step & Purpose\\\hline
\(U,c\) & Add \(v\) and use the positive distance from closed \(X\).\\
Define \(z\) & Zero on \(X\), nonzero at \(v\).\\
Bound \(z\) & Infimum yields \(\|z\|\leq1\).\\
Hahn--Banach & Extend to \(y\in V^*\).\\\hline
\end{tabularx}}

\part*{Topic K: Spectral Radius Limit Formula}

\section*{Definitions for understanding}
Let \(V\neq\{0\}\) be a finite-dimensional normed complex vector space and \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\).
The \emph{spectral radius} (Definition 13.1) is
\[
 \boxed{\rho(A)=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\lambda|,}
\]
where \(\sigma(A)\) is the set of eigenvalues. Over \(\mathbb C\), the spectrum is nonempty because the characteristic polynomial splits.
The \emph{operator norm} is
\[
 \|A\|=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|=\sup_{v\neq0}\frac{\|Av\|}{\|v\|}.
\]
It measures how much \(A\) can stretch a vector.
Useful results from the notes are
\[
 \rho(A)\leq\|A\|\tag{13.1}
 \qquad \rho(\alpha A)=|\alpha|\rho(A)\tag{13.2}
\]
and, when \(A\) is orthogonally diagonalizable with respect to the inner product inducing the operator norm,
\[
 \rho(A)=\|A\|.\tag{13.3}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why (13.1)?} If \(Av=\lambda v\) with \(v\neq0\), then
\(|\lambda|\|v\|=\|Av\|\leq\|A\|\|v\|\), so \(|\lambda|\leq\|A\|\); maximize over eigenvalues.

\textbf{Why (13.2)?} Scaling \(A\) by \(\alpha\) scales every eigenvalue by \(\alpha\), so the largest absolute value is multiplied by \(|\alpha|\). For \(\alpha=0\), both sides are zero.
\end{personalnote}

\section{Theorem 13.2: Gelfand's limit formula}
\textbf{Statement.} For every \(A\in\operatorname{End}(V)\),
\[
 \boxed{\rho(A)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\|A^k\|^{1/k}.}
\]
Although \(\|A\|\) depends on the choice of norm, this limit is the spectral radius.

\section{Lemma 13.3: Lower bound}
\textbf{Statement.} For every positive integer \(k\),
\[
 \boxed{\rho(A)\leq\|A^k\|^{1/k}.}
\]
\textbf{Proof, following the lecture notes.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item Rewrite the claim as \(\rho(A)^k\leq\|A^k\|\), since taking positive \(k\)-th roots preserves inequalities.
\item If \(Av=\lambda v\), \(v\neq0\), then
\[
 A^2v=A(\lambda v)=\lambda Av=\lambda^2v,\quad\ldots,\quad
 A^kv=\lambda^kv.
\]
Thus \(\lambda^k\in\sigma(A^k)\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why does the scalar pass through \(A\)?} By linearity, \(A(\lambda v)=\lambda A(v)\). We repeat this \(k\) times; no commutation between different operators is needed.
\end{personalnote}
\item Therefore
\[
 \rho(A)^k=\max_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}|\lambda|^k
 \leq\max_{\mu\in\sigma(A^k)}|\mu|
 =\rho(A^k)\leq\|A^k\|.
\]
The middle inequality holds since the powers of eigenvalues of \(A\) occur among the eigenvalues of \(A^k\); the last inequality is (13.1). Take \(k\)-th roots.
\end{proofsteps}

\section{Lemma 13.4: If \(\rho(A)<1\), then \(A^k\to0\)}
\textbf{Statement.} If \(\rho(A)<1\), then \(A^k\to0\) as \(k\to\infty\), in operator norm.

\textbf{Proof, following the lecture notes.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item \textbf{Jordan decomposition.} By Theorem 9.5,
\[
 \boxed{A=A_d+A_n,\qquad A_dA_n=A_nA_d,}
\]
with \(A_d\) diagonalizable and \(A_n\) nilpotent. Also
\(\sigma(A_d)=\sigma(A)\), so \(\rho(A_d)=\rho(A)<1\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Does Theorem 9.5 require a splitting minimal polynomial?} Yes; here \(V\) is over \(\mathbb C\), and the fundamental theorem of algebra guarantees that the minimal polynomial splits. The decomposition gives commutativity, which will be important for the binomial formula. Nilpotent means \(A_n^m=0\) for some \(m\geq1\).
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Choose a convenient norm.} Because \(V\) is finite-dimensional, all norms are equivalent (Theorem 11.8), so convergence does not depend on which one we choose. Since \(A_d\) is diagonalizable, choose an eigenvector basis \(e_1,\ldots,e_n\) and \emph{declare it orthonormal} by defining
\[
 \left\langle\sum_i\alpha_ie_i,\sum_i\beta_ie_i\right\rangle
 =\sum_i\alpha_i\overline{\beta_i}.
\]
Use the corresponding vector norm \(\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}\) and its associated operator norm.
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why may we do this?} A basis provides unique coordinates; the usual complex dot product on their coordinate lists is a valid inner product. The formula follows from \(\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}\). The \emph{associated operator norm} is the operator norm computed using this particular vector norm. Since all norms are equivalent in finite dimensions, it is enough to prove convergence in this one.
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Use the special norm.} In our new inner product the eigenvector basis is orthonormal, so \(A_d\) is orthogonally diagonalizable. From (13.3),
\[
 \boxed{\|A_d\|=\rho(A_d)=\rho(A)<1.}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why introduce an inner product?} We specifically need \(\|A_d\|<1\). In an arbitrary norm, \(\rho(A_d)<1\) does not necessarily imply \(\|A_d\|<1\). This basis-dependent inner product makes the two equal.
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Binomial expansion.} Let \(m\) be the nilpotency index of \(A_n\), so \(A_n^j=0\) for \(j\geq m\). Because \(A_d\) and \(A_n\) commute, for \(k\geq m\)
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 A^k&=(A_d+A_n)^k
 =\sum_{j=0}^k\binom{k}{j}A_d^{k-j}A_n^j\\
 &=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{k}{j}A_d^{k-j}A_n^j.
 \end{aligned}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is commutativity needed?} Without \(A_dA_n=A_nA_d\), terms in the product cannot be collected as binomial coefficients. For example, \((B+C)^2=B^2+BC+CB+C^2\), not \(B^2+2BC+C^2\) unless \(BC=CB\).

\textbf{Why stop at \(m-1\)?} Every term with \(j\geq m\) contains \(A_n^j=A_n^mA_n^{j-m}=0\).
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Estimate the norm.} The triangle inequality gives
\[
 \|A^k\|
 \leq\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{k}{j}\|A_d^{k-j}A_n^j\|.
\]
For \(0\leq j\leq m-1\), \(k\geq m\),
\[
 \binom{k}{j}=\frac{k(k-1)\cdots(k-j+1)}{j!}
 \leq k^j\leq k^m.
\]
And \(k-j=(k-m)+(m-j)\), so
\[
 A_d^{k-j}=A_d^{k-m}A_d^{m-j}.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \|A^k\|
 &\leq k^m\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\|A_d^{k-m}A_d^{m-j}A_n^j\|\\
 &\leq k^m\|A_d^{k-m}\|
       \sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\|A_d^{m-j}A_n^j\|\\
 &\leq Ck^m\|A_d\|^{k-m},
 \end{aligned}
\]
where
\(
 C:=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\|A_d^{m-j}A_n^j\|
\)
does not depend on \(k\).
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why may we pull out \(k-m\)?} Submultiplicativity,
\(\|BC\|\leq\|B\|\|C\|\), bounds each summand by
\(\|A_d^{k-m}\|\|A_d^{m-j}A_n^j\|\).
The first factor does not depend on \(j\); hence it can be placed outside the sum. Also \(\|A_d^{k-m}\|\leq\|A_d\|^{k-m}\).

\textbf{Why \(\binom{k}{j}\leq k^m\)?} The numerator has \(j\) factors, each at most \(k\), while \(j!\geq1\); and \(j\leq m\), \(k\geq1\).
\end{personalnote}

\item \textbf{Convergence.} Let \(q=\|A_d\|<1\).
If \(q=0\), then \(A_d=0\) and \(A=A_n\) is nilpotent, hence \(A^k=0\) for \(k\geq m\).
If \(0<q<1\), then \(k^mq^{k-m}\to0\): exponential decay beats polynomial growth. In either case,
\[
 \boxed{\|A^k\|\longrightarrow0.}
\]
Because norms are equivalent in finite dimensions, this establishes convergence in the originally given norm as well.
\end{proofsteps}

\section{Proof of Theorem 13.2: Spectral radius limit formula}
\textbf{Proof, following the lecture notes.}
\begin{proofsteps}
\item By Lemma 13.3, for every positive integer \(k\),
\[
 \rho(A)\leq\|A^k\|^{1/k}.
\]
\item Let \(\varepsilon>0\) and put \(\tau=\rho(A)+\varepsilon\), so \(\tau>\rho(A)\).
Set \(B=A/\tau\). By (13.2),
\[
 \boxed{\rho(B)=\rho(A)/\tau<1.}
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why divide by \(\tau\)?} We want the hypothesis of Lemma 13.4, namely spectral radius strictly below one. Eigenvalues of \(A/\tau\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\) divided by \(\tau\), so this choice guarantees it.
\end{personalnote}
\item Apply Lemma 13.4 to \(B\):
\[
 B^k=\frac{A^k}{\tau^k}\longrightarrow0,
 \qquad
 \frac{\|A^k\|}{\tau^k}\longrightarrow0.
\]
For all sufficiently large \(k\), this nonnegative quantity is at most \(1\). Consequently,
\[
 \|A^k\|\leq\tau^k
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 \|A^k\|^{1/k}\leq\tau.
\]
\begin{personalnote}
\textbf{Why is it eventually \(\leq1\)?} By the definition of convergence to zero, with tolerance \(1\), the nonnegative sequence \(\|A^k/\tau^k\|\) is eventually less than \(1\). This yields the desired upper bound.
\end{personalnote}
\item Combine this with Lemma 13.3:
\[
 \boxed{\rho(A)\leq\|A^k\|^{1/k}\leq\tau=\rho(A)+\varepsilon}
\]
for all sufficiently large \(k\).
Thus
\(
0\leq\|A^k\|^{1/k}-\rho(A)\leq\varepsilon
\)
eventually, for \emph{every} \(\varepsilon>0\). This is precisely convergence to \(\rho(A)\), proving
\[
 \boxed{\lim_{k\to\infty}\|A^k\|^{1/k}=\rho(A).}
\]
\end{proofsteps}
\prooftable{\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.26\linewidth}X@{}}\hline
Step & Purpose\\\hline
Lemma 13.3 & Lower bound \(\rho(A)\).\\
Rescale & Force \(\rho(A/\tau)<1\).\\
Lemma 13.4 & Obtain the eventual upper bound \(\|A^k\|^{1/k}\leq\tau\).\\
Squeeze & Let the arbitrary \(\varepsilon\) tend to zero.\\\hline
\end{tabularx}}

\section*{Big Picture: Topic K}
The spectral radius measures the largest absolute eigenvalue, while the operator norm measures maximal stretching. The first is always bounded by the second but they need not be equal. Repeated powers reveal the spectral radius: Lemma 13.3 gives a lower bound, Lemma 13.4 supplies decay after rescaling, and an \(\varepsilon\)-squeeze proves the limit formula.
